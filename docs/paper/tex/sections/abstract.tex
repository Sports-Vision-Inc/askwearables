\begin{abstract}
\noindent
People ask general-purpose AI agents about their own sleep, heart rate and training, and today the agent either
reads the wearable record in its context or fetches whatever an unrestricted tool server returns. Both designs
leave three jobs to the language model: computing over the numbers, deciding what to disclose, and answering the
same way twice. We present \sys{}, a broker that takes those jobs away from the agent model. An LLM planner that
never sees health data turns each question into a typed query over a tested metric layer; a policy gate written as
plain code checks the query against a per-agent, per-purpose grant and releases the coarsest of four levels that
answers it; and every agent receives the same short packet, with a hash-chained receipt for every release and
denial. We compare \sys{} with raw context and with open tools, holding the agent model fixed, across \nModels{}
models and \nTasks{} questions over a synthetic cohort whose ground truth is known by construction and which contains
the data-quality traps of real wearable data. With \sys{}, accuracy ranged from \accCMin{} to \accCMax{} across the
models, against \accAMin{} to \accAMax{} with raw context and \accBMin{} to \accBMax{} with open
tools; the pooled gain over raw context was \bootPooledCAEst{} points (\bootPooledCACI{}, from a bootstrap over
people). \sys{} was more accurate than raw context for \nSigCA{} of \nTestedCA{} models (exact McNemar, $p<0.05$) and
significantly less accurate for none; for the strongest models the differences were small (at most four points). On 152
questions over real Fitbit data, with the six open-weight models, \sys{} raised accuracy for the four models that were weakest with raw context and
lowered it for the two that were strongest. We
name handing the model more of the record than the question needs \emph{context over-exposure} and measure it apart
from leakage: raw context put \expRatioAC{} times as much of the record in front of the model as \sys{}; open tools
delivered a similar volume but could reach the whole record. Under a leak detector checked against hand labels,
\sys{} leaked in \leakPooledC{} of privacy runs, against \leakPooledA{} for raw context and \leakPooledB{} for open
tools; for data outside a grant this is enforced by construction. On PHIA's objective questions, \sys{} answered \code{unknown} to \phiaUnsupPooledC{} of the questions it does not
support, against \phiaUnsupPooledA{} for raw context, but was less accurate on the answerable ones
(\phiaWordedPooledC{} against \phiaWordedPooledA{}); its two largest causes of error lay in the broker's design
rather than in the agent models: threshold counts were released as yes or no, and a metric the planner chose fell
outside the grant. Changing both, as the first of two pre-registered steps from the study to the deployed broker,
brought pooled accuracy on fresh answerable PHIA questions, as worded, level with raw context for the open-weight models
(\vtwoWordedPooledC{} against \vtwoWordedPooledA{}; \vtwoStudyWordedC{} against \vtwoStudyWordedA{} on the study's
questions), though not for every model and not on PHIA's own labels. The second step, which gave the planner the comparison windows,
removed a regression on one synthetic question and raised accuracy on real data from \vthreeRealOne{} to
\vthreeRealThree{} (not pre-registered), without changing the PHIA results.
\end{abstract}
